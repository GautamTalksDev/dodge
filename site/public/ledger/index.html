How many of Starlink's collision-avoidance burns can public orbit data see?

SpaceX told the FCC its satellites made 207,152 collision-avoidance manoeuvres from 1 December 2025 to 31 May 2026: about 42 per satellite per year. We looked for them in 14.5 days of public element sets. In the cleanest test, at most 8% of them can be 16 cm/s or larger, the size public data reliably sees.

The one-minute check

42.1avoidance manoeuvres per satellite per year, filed by SpaceX (65,137 Gen 1 plus 142,015 Gen 2, over an average of 9,866 Starlink satellites in orbit)
2.8at most, per satellite per year: burns of 16 cm/s or more on satellites holding 530 to 600 km (conservative 95% bound from 6 steps of any kind in 4.8 satellite-years)
8%of even the lower filed rate (Gen 1, about 36 per satellite per year) could be burns that large

So, if the filing-period rate still held, at least 92% of Starlink avoidance manoeuvres change the orbit by less than 16 cm/s along track, or leave its size unchanged. Public element sets therefore cannot rebuild the 207,152. We did not reproduce it; this page shows why it cannot be reproduced this way.

Detection limit in that band: the 175 m threshold is about 9.5 cm/s at its median altitude of 570 km; a burn of 16 cm/s added to a real recorded history is found 98% of the time; 10 cm/s, 65%. False alarms on passive debris: 0.6 per object per year (95% upper 2.1).

How the bound is built: the upper end of the two-sided 95% Poisson interval for the step count (one-sided, that end has 97.5% coverage), divided by the lower end of the 95% interval for recall (also one-sided 97.5%). Two bounds combined this way are conservative: by Bonferroni, joint coverage is at least about 95%.

What the steps in public data are

Across 9,479 satellites holding altitude between 400 and 600 km (113 satellite-years observed), steps above 175 m of semi-major axis (about 9.7 cm/s at 470 km) happen 25.2 times per satellite per year as drag make-up boosts (a decay, then a boost back) and 8.7 times as other small single changes (95% interval 8.2 to 9.3). Both are far rarer in the highest band, where drag is weakest. Close approaches are not: DODGE's daily screen of the same satellites finds more passes under 1 km in the highest band than in the busiest one. So most visible steps follow drag, not conjunctions.

Detection limit

Each point: 570 to 585 burns injected into randomly chosen real on-station histories, or synthetic histories with fit noise of 12.9 m and a bad-set share of 0.3%, both measured on the real data. Synthetic precision at 16 cm/s: 100.0%.

Rate against threshold

The threshold is the smallest step on the grid at which the 95% upper bound of false alarms on passive debris near Starlink altitudes is at most 5% of the filed rate. The rule is fixed in code; the threshold follows from the debris data alone. Zero false alarms are drawn on the bottom edge of the log scale.

Limits, stated first

  • Different months: the filing covers 1 December 2025 to 31 May 2026; the data covers 3 October 2026 to 17 October 2026. Rates are compared, not counts. The 92% figure assumes satellites in the highest band dodge about as often as the Gen 1 fleet did then; DODGE's screen shows them with more close approaches, not fewer. If they dodged only a tenth as often, up to 78% of their burns could be that large.
  • Only the size of the orbit is measured. Two burns that cancel inside one gap between element sets, or burns that change only the plane or shape, are invisible.
  • The classes are heuristics. Drag make-up and avoidance cannot be told apart for a single step; the bound uses every step in the band, whatever its class.
  • Public GP sets are derived from US Space Surveillance Network radar and optical observations (CelesTrak's description); whether they also use operator ephemerides is not known to us.
  • The highest band holds 298 satellites. The bound tightens as DODGE keeps recording every 4 hours.

What an insider could check in a minute

  1. Is a typical Starlink avoidance burn smaller than 16 cm/s along track?
  2. Are the regular boosts every few days in the lower shells drag make-up, as classed here?
  3. Do SpaceX's published ephemerides include planned burns? Our GP and SupGP steps agree less often than we pre-registered.
